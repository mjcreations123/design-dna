# Positive art direction

Use this for greenfield work, redesigns, or any change that establishes a visual premise.

## Start from meaning

1. Name the audience, task, decision, and desired feeling.
2. Inventory the real material: product behavior, language, place, people, objects, data, constraints, and approved brand assets.
3. Identify the category mean without treating it as forbidden.
4. Form two or three materially different directions from the real material.
5. Choose for fit, clarity, credibility, access, feasibility, and maintainability.
6. Prove the direction with real copy and a representative component before scaling it.

A direction is a relationship between content, composition, type, color, imagery, and behavior. It is not a style label or a list of effects.

## Translate evidence into a system

Use a traceable chain rather than a mood-board label:

> A repair service receives most requests while people are standing beside a
> failed appliance. That condition favors direct task labels, large tap targets,
> short diagnostic branches, persistent recovery information, and restrained
> media that does not compete with instructions. The direction is not
> “industrial”; it is calm action under household stress.

This example is a method, not a visual recipe. For the current project, connect
each consequential choice to supplied material:

| Evidence | User consequence | System decision | Proof |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Real behavior, language, place, object, data, or constraint | What it changes about understanding, trust, or action | Type, color, composition, content, media, or behavior relationship | Representative copy, state, interaction, and narrow transformation |

If the evidence could be swapped for an unrelated project without changing the
system, the direction is still too generic.

Do not turn this skill's terminology, a favorite palette, a sequence of
full-ground “acts,” one hero silhouette, or a recurring signature interaction
into a replacement house style. Internal rationale may name a premise; the
public result and production source should express the project rather than
narrate the design method.

## Set the ambition

Define the project's own ambition in plain language. The registers below are
non-ordered examples, not category mappings or a ladder from safe to bold. A
project may combine them when the material and task explain the combination.

| Example register | Conditions that can justify it | Quality lever | Failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional and quiet | Repetition, urgency, precision, or reference use | Density, language, states, speed | Looking unfinished |
| Refined and editorial | Reading, comparison, material detail, or considered pacing | Rhythm, hierarchy, imagery, copy | Generic luxury |
| Expressive and brand-led | A supplied voice, visual premise, or recognition goal | Specific composition and system | Trend collage |
| Immersive and experiential | Sequence, space, transformation, or exploration matters to understanding | Meaningful interaction and continuity | Spectacle without purpose |

Do not force memorability through motion, novelty, asymmetry, texture, or decoration. A precise content model or unusually clear workflow can be the signature.

## Calibrate the time register

Describe the intended relationship to time in the project's own words. Terms such
as current, forward-looking, heritage, archival, or intentionally timeless are
examples, not a closed taxonomy. For public greenfield work:

- inspect a small set of current, dated category peers;
- inspect adjacent fields for transferable ideas;
- name the dated shorthand and current fashionable default that would weaken this project;
- test whether the result could be mistaken for a generic template from another year;
- prefer durable relationships over trend ingredients.

Do not infer nostalgia from category. Do not replace an old cliché with a new one such as cream-plus-display-serif, dark-neon glass, or a stock product-dashboard hero.
Do not let every conspicuous choice restate one category adjective such as
“industrial,” “premium,” or “technical.” Type, color, imagery, structure, and
behavior should earn their roles from different relevant evidence.

## Protect cultural specificity

- Ask what local conventions, languages, densities, symbols, or visual practices matter.
- Treat regional preferences as design evidence, not noise to normalize.
- Do not use culture, disability, age, gender, family, work, or place as costume.
- Verify unfamiliar symbols and imagery with a knowledgeable source or owner.
- Support RTL, translation expansion, and locale-specific reading behavior when relevant.

## Pressure-test the premise

Before committing, ask:

- Would this direction still make sense without its trendiest effect?
- Which project fact explains each conspicuous choice?
- Does it improve the task or only decorate it?
- Is the result recognizable as this project with its logo removed?
- Do distinct routes follow their own entry questions and decision
  dependencies, or reuse one dramatic silhouette?
- If the font, palette, or trendiest effect is removed in turn, does real
  project specificity remain in content, structure, media, and behavior?
- Are familiar conventions preserved where familiarity protects the user?
- Can the system extend across routes, states, content lengths, and devices?

Reject directions that depend on invented proof, inaccessible behavior, fragile assets, or imitation of one reference.
