# Public copy and voice

Use this for visitor-facing headings, introductions, body copy, marketing
language, editorial framing, and longer explanations. [Content and
IA](content-ia.md) decides what information belongs and where;
[microcopy](microcopy.md) handles functional strings; [parseable
text](../quality/parseable-text.md) decides whether a visible string has a
public job.

The goal is language that sounds like this organization speaking usefully to
this audience. It is not an AI-authorship detector and it is not a punctuation
or sentence-shape blacklist.

## Resolve the writing authority

Before drafting, identify:

- the approved facts, claims, terminology, quotations, and legal boundaries;
- the existing brand or author voice worth preserving;
- the intended reader, entry context, likely knowledge, and next decision;
- the surface and genre: campaign, local business, product, publication,
  documentation, dashboard, cultural work, or another real context;
- the factual owner and the person authorized to approve voice.

Project voice outranks a generic clarity style. When no approved voice exists,
write plainly and provisionally from known facts rather than manufacturing a
personality. Technical-documentation conventions can inform a help page without
turning a campaign, restaurant, arts publication, or luxury brand into developer
documentation.

For Standard-or-stronger public builds, read
[Public copy integrity](../quality/public-copy-integrity.md) before drafting
prominent public language. It adds a source/anchor and rendered-corpus closure;
it does not replace this voice process with a generic anti-AI phrase list.

Preserve every confirmed claim and the source's intended meaning. Do not add a
metric, customer, affiliation, superlative, mechanism, promise, example, or
emotion because it makes the prose sound more complete.

## Draft from evidence and reader consequence

- Start with the subject, offer, fact, scene, action, or tension the reader
  actually came for. Remove a preamble that merely announces that the page is
  about to explain something.
- Prefer concrete nouns and observable verbs where they make the meaning
  clearer. Name who does what when the actor matters; passive voice remains
  valid when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally de-emphasized.
- Put a condition or safety limit before the instruction or promise it changes.
- Use second person when the reader's action or consequence is central. Do not
  force it into editorial, institutional, legal, or collective language where
  another person fits better.
- Use the audience's real terminology consistently. Explain necessary jargon;
  do not replace meaningful specialist language with vague friendliness.
- Prefer sentence case for ordinary interface and explanatory text when no
  approved brand, editorial, language, or platform convention says otherwise.
- Write for the real geographic and cultural audience. Avoid an unexplained
  idiom, joke, or reference when it would exclude readers the project intends
  to serve.

A useful sentence contributes at least one real job: fact, orientation,
distinction, proof, consequence, instruction, invitation, atmosphere, voice, or
transition. Atmosphere and play are legitimate jobs when they belong to the
direction; they do not need to masquerade as information.

## Keep construction language private

An owner's “because” clause, prompt, creative rationale, content-model field,
backend category, workflow stage, test name, component label, and agent note are
inputs to the work. They are not public copy merely because they are available.
Translate a needed internal state into the true visitor consequence. Otherwise,
let the reasoning shape hierarchy, media, and behavior without narrating it.

This boundary includes explanatory copy that tells visitors why the designer
used a large photograph, quiet palette, unusual layout, or visual metaphor. A
public design explanation is valid only when the brief explicitly calls for it
or the visitor needs it to understand the subject, act, or avoid a concrete
misconception.

## Diagnose producer patterns after the draft

Run this pass only after real copy exists. One pattern is a prompt to inspect,
not a failure. Look for clusters and causes:

- portable praise or promise language that could move unchanged to an
  unrelated organization;
- an empty opening such as a welcome, throat-clearing summary, or announcement
  of what the next sentence will say;
- abstract benefit words where a verified mechanism, object, example, or
  consequence is available;
- repeated “not just X, but Y,” “whether you are X or Y,” rhetorical questions,
  polished contrasts, reassurance, or groups of three across unrelated
  sections;
- vague attribution such as “experts say,” “many believe,” or “research shows”
  without a usable source;
- synonym cycling that renames the same product, person, or action to avoid
  repetition and quietly changes its meaning;
- chatbot residue: answering an objection nobody raised, congratulating the
  reader, narrating the response, offering extra examples, or explaining the
  obvious after the point is settled;
- every section using the same sentence length, heading grammar, proof cadence,
  and concluding action regardless of its job;
- a generic final uplift or call to action that does not follow from the page's
  actual decision.

Do not turn these observations into a banned-word list. Em dashes, adverbs,
parentheses, passive voice, fragments, metaphors, questions, repetition, and
long or short sentences can all be right. A rigid anti-slop dialect becomes a
new recognizable template and can erase a client's real voice.

Never claim that a pattern proves who or what wrote the text.

## Edit without flattening the voice

Use the minimum effective edit:

1. preserve the claim, intent, register, rhythm, and intentional oddities;
2. remove the sentence that has no public job;
3. replace portable filler with a sourced fact, mechanism, consequence, image,
   scene, or next action when one exists;
4. vary a repeated construction because the section jobs differ, not merely to
   manufacture irregularity;
5. read the passage aloud and revise the point where a human reader would lose
   the subject, antecedent, breath, or reason to continue.

Do not “humanize” copy by adding typos, fake uncertainty, arbitrary first-person
opinions, slang, mess, or contrarian claims. Do not sand away culturally or
professionally meaningful language just to make a detector score change.

## Review the rendered copy

- Read navigation, headings, captions, and actions without body text; the
  route's promise and sequence should still be intelligible.
- Read the body aloud and mark sentences whose contribution cannot be named.
- Compare repeated sections and routes for portable grammar, not only repeated
  words.
- Inspect the actual render for wrapping, truncation, hierarchy, line length,
  contrast, and whether a decorative label or layout changes the apparent
  meaning.
- Ask an unprimed reviewer what the organization does, what is distinctive,
  what is proven, and what they can do next. Record confusion as evidence; do
  not rewrite a factual source merely to force a smoother answer.
- Recheck every changed claim with its source and owner.

The pass is complete when the copy is truthful, visitor-useful, specific to the
project, recognizable in its approved voice, and well set in the final render.
It does not certify human authorship or universal taste.
