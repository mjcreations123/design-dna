# Layout and density

Use this when establishing grids, spacing, section rhythm, or responsive composition.

## Build a content-driven structure

- Start with information relationships and task sequence.
- Select a grid that supports the content; do not begin from a fashionable silhouette.
- Define a small spacing scale and a few purposeful container widths.
- Use proximity to group, alignment to relate, and whitespace to separate.
- Let density follow task frequency, complexity, device, and user expertise.
- Keep important controls near the content or state they affect.

Do not equate large whitespace with quality or high density with clutter. Operational tools may need compact, scannable layouts; an editorial feature may need breathing room.

## Create rhythm without repetition

Vary section pace when the content changes role:

- orient;
- explain;
- compare;
- prove;
- act;
- recover or continue.

Avoid assigning the same heading block, padding, and equal-card grid to every role. Also avoid random alternation introduced only to appear custom.

## Use optical judgment

- Align text by its visible edge, not always its box.
- Balance icons and labels by perceived weight.
- Account for image focal points when cropping and centering.
- Keep repeated items mechanically consistent unless a priority difference is real.
- Encode recurring corrections in component variants or tokens.

## Stress-test the layout

Inspect continuously while resizing, including widths between named breakpoints. Test:

- very short and very long headings;
- missing and unusually tall media;
- translation expansion and RTL;
- browser zoom and user text enlargement;
- sparse, typical, and dense data;
- portrait, landscape, split-screen, and short-height windows;
- sticky or fixed elements with safe areas and virtual keyboards;
- scroll containers, tables, dialogs, and menus at their worst plausible size.

## Diagnose common failure

Look for:

- almost-aligned edges and unexplained one-off offsets;
- fixed heights that clip real content;
- horizontal overflow hidden rather than solved;
- controls separated from their labels, results, or consequences;
- uniformly huge vertical padding;
- type scaled beyond the space needed for comprehension;
- mobile layouts that merely stack desktop boxes;
- wide layouts that stretch reading lines or scatter related content.

Prefer structural fixes over breakpoint patches. Add a breakpoint only when content or behavior actually needs to change.
