# Design-partner cadence

Use this optionally for durable products and services where a team has ongoing
ownership, recurring access to appropriate participants, and decisions that
benefit from learning across releases. Do not impose it on a one-off site,
short campaign, or project without a responsible research and delivery owner.

Design partners are not a preference panel, approval committee, beta-marketing
list, or substitute for broader research. They are people with relevant lived
or working context who can expose assumptions, co-create possibilities, and
evaluate changes over time.

## Define the partnership

Record:

- the product outcome and decisions the partnership may inform;
- participant context, represented and absent audiences, and relevant access
  needs;
- recruitment basis, consent, compensation, privacy, retention, and withdrawal
  arrangements;
- product, research, accessibility, and decision owners;
- what partners can influence and what policy, safety, legal, technical, or
  organizational constraints are fixed;
- how findings, decisions, follow-up, and closure will be communicated.

Avoid dependence on one unusually available customer or stakeholder. Do not
present recurring participants as representative of an entire market or
population.

## Set a proportional cadence

Choose touchpoints around meaningful decisions and risk rather than a universal
calendar quota. A cadence may include:

- discovery when the problem, context, or service boundary is uncertain;
- co-creation while viable models are still open;
- playback when a direction, workflow, or system relationship needs challenge;
- evaluative sessions when realistic behavior can answer a specific question;
- post-release review when actual use, support, or outcome evidence exists.

Every session should name the decision it can change, the artifact or build
being discussed, known constraints, method, owner, and follow-up date. Skip or
combine ceremonies that cannot change a decision.

## Separate evidence types

Record contributions according to what they establish:

- `context evidence`: a participant's real situation, workflow, constraint, or
  experience;
- `observed behavior`: what happened during a realistic task or use;
- `co-created possibility`: an idea produced together for further evaluation;
- `feature request`: a requested solution or capability, not proof of broad
  need or the correct design;
- `preference`: a stated taste, not observed usability or outcome evidence;
- `operational fact`: a claim verified against an accountable system, policy,
  owner, or service record;
- `hypothesis`: an interpretation that still needs testing.

Preserve the original type when synthesizing. Do not convert repeated feature
requests into validated requirements without establishing the underlying need,
affected audience, consequence, feasibility, and competing evidence.

## Run useful sessions

Give participants enough realistic context to respond, but do not reveal the
desired answer. Observe behavior before asking for opinion when the question is
evaluative. In co-creation, make constraints visible and explore the underlying
problem before converging on interface details.

Capture:

- participant code and relevant context without unnecessary personal data;
- artifact, route, prototype, or exact build version;
- prompt, scenario, task, and available information;
- observation, participant interpretation, idea, and facilitator inference as
  separate fields;
- conflicting evidence, access barriers, and people not represented;
- decision made, deferred, or left open, with owner and rationale.

Use the restricted user-validation record when observations or personal data
need durable storage.

## Prevent design by vote

Do not ask partners to select a winner by majority vote or average several
coherent directions into one. Preference counts do not establish usability,
accessibility, truth, feasibility, business value, or population-wide demand.

The accountable decision owner should synthesize:

- user outcomes and observed breakdowns;
- strength, relevance, and limits of the evidence;
- the product and brand thesis;
- accessibility, service, policy, safety, technical, and commercial
  constraints;
- qualities worth protecting from the approved direction;
- consequences and reversibility of the decision.

When feedback conflicts, preserve the disagreement and investigate the cause:
different contexts, access needs, mental models, roles, maturity, or incentives
may require a conditional design rather than a compromised average.

## Close the learning loop

After a touchpoint:

1. return a concise account of what was heard and what remains uncertain;
2. record what changed, what did not, and why;
3. bind consequential decisions to the evaluated artifact or build;
4. re-test material changes when risk warrants it;
5. review production outcomes, support evidence, and unintended effects;
6. tell partners how their contribution affected the work when consent and the
   relationship permit it.

Periodically verify that the partnership still represents relevant contexts,
that participation is not burdensome or extractive, and that the cadence is
changing decisions. Pause or end it when it no longer has a clear decision
purpose, safe research basis, accountable owner, or credible route to action.
